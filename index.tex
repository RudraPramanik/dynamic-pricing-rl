% ── Step 1: Abstract ─────────────────────────────────────────
\begin{abstract}
Retail dynamic pricing requires both demand structure and sequential
adaptivity; purely econometric and purely model-free reinforcement-learning
(RL) methods address complementary aspects of this problem.
We describe \textit{EG-DQN} (Econometric-Guided Deep Q-Network), a hybrid
agent that incorporates Generalized Linear Model~(GLM) demand signals into
a Deep Q-Network through state augmentation, econometric reward shaping,
and early action masking.
We evaluate the design in a controlled simulation of three substitute
products over 1{,}460~days of synthetic daily demand, with a 120-day
holdout on Item~1 and comparison of fixed-price, rule-based, GLM-optimal,
pure DQN, and EG-DQN policies.
On the holdout window, GLM-optimal pricing and EG-DQN attain identical
cumulative revenue and select the same price on the discrete grid; a
one-sided Mann--Whitney test does not indicate higher daily revenue for
EG-DQN relative to the GLM benchmark ($p=0.50$).
Relative to fixed-price and rule-based baselines, EG-DQN yields higher
holdout revenue in this simulation (Mann--Whitney $p<0.001$ in both
comparisons).
Pure DQN attains lower holdout revenue than GLM-optimal and EG-DQN under
the learned policies.
An ablation suggests that econometric reward shaping accounts for the
principal holdout improvement over pure DQN, while the full hybrid does
not exceed a reward-only configuration on test revenue.
Training-episode rewards are modestly higher for EG-DQN than for pure DQN.
These findings are confined to a synthetic, per-item, discrete-action
environment with partial alignment between the data-generating process
and the GLM specification; operational deployment is not claimed.
\end{abstract}

% ── Step 2: Introduction ─────────────────────────────────────
\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Retail managers set prices under incomplete information.
When tomorrow's price is chosen, competitor reactions, seasonal
shifts, and the price sensitivity of the current customer pool
are not directly observable.
Mistakes are costly: a price set too low forfeits revenue that
cannot be recovered, and a price set too high suppresses demand
immediately.

The literature has developed two complementary approaches.
Econometric methods estimate price-response relationships from
historical transactions, yielding interpretable parameters---in
particular, own-price elasticity---that managers can audit and reconcile with
demand theory~\cite{kopalle2023}.
Their optimisation is typically open-loop and rests on assumptions
such as relatively stable demand relationships over the estimation
window.
Reinforcement learning~(RL), by contrast, treats pricing as a
sequential decision problem in which an agent learns a policy from
repeated market interaction~\cite{groeneveld2025}.
RL adapts to feedback without committing to a fixed parametric form,
yet retail applications must contend with sample-efficient learning,
noisy rewards, and exploration over large price sets~\cite{apte2024,
cheung2023}.

Apte~et~al.~\cite{apte2024} show that tabular Q-learning can improve
on static pricing in simulated retail settings.
Groeneveld~et~al.~\cite{groeneveld2025} report that RL remains
competitive under demand-model misspecification but can learn more
slowly when demand structure is known---the regime in which
econometric guidance may be most informative.
When live experimentation is impractical, simulation-based evaluation
offers a controlled alternative~\cite{nambiar2022, chen2023}.
We adopt that evaluation mode here; operational deployment is not
claimed.

We describe \textit{EG-DQN} (Econometric-Guided Deep Q-Network),
which couples a Generalized Linear Model~(GLM) demand specification
with a Deep Q-Network~(DQN) pricing agent.
GLM-derived signals enter the agent through three mechanisms:
state augmentation with the GLM-optimal price at each timestep,
econometric reward shaping, and early action masking during
exploration.
The design is assessed in a synthetic environment with three
substitute products, 1{,}460~days of daily demand, and a 120-day
holdout on Item~1; we compare fixed-price, rule-based, GLM-optimal,
pure DQN, and EG-DQN policies using holdout revenue, Mann--Whitney
tests, and a three-configuration ablation.

\subsection*{Contributions}

\begin{itemize}
  \item We \textbf{describe} EG-DQN and three mechanisms for
    injecting GLM demand signals---optimal-price guidance, reward
    shaping, and masked exploration---into DQN-based retail pricing.
  \item We \textbf{implement} a reproducible simulation benchmark
    with known elasticities, five baseline strategies, holdout
    evaluation on Item~1, non-parametric significance testing, and
    ablation of econometric reward shaping.
  \item We \textbf{report} that GLM-optimal and EG-DQN policies
    coincide on holdout revenue in this simulation while exceeding
    naive fixed-price and rule-based strategies; econometric reward
    shaping aligns RL outcomes with GLM-guided prices, and EG-DQN
    attains modest training-phase gains over pure DQN, without
    claiming holdout dominance over econometric pricing alone.
\end{itemize}

% ── Step 3: Related Work ─────────────────────────────────────
\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Dynamic Pricing and RL}

Chenavaz and Dimitrov~\cite{chenavaz2025} review how artificial
intelligence methods have entered dynamic pricing research across
operations and applied economics.
Kopalle~et~al.~\cite{kopalle2023} summarise managerial uses of dynamic
pricing and the econometric questions that remain open in retail
practice.
On the algorithmic side, Groeneveld~et~al.~\cite{groeneveld2025}
compare reinforcement learning with data-driven dynamic programming
in finite-horizon markets; Razumovskiy and Karenin~\cite{razumovskiy2025}
report a related finite-horizon comparison of fitted dynamic programming
and RL.
Liu~et~al.~\cite{liu2024} extend deep RL to joint omni-channel pricing
and inventory control when demand is uncertain.
Li and Zheng~\cite{xiaocheng2023} study pricing under inventory
constraints and external information within an online learning
framework---a reminder that demand estimation and sequential price
setting are seldom separable in applied settings.

\subsection{Demand Forecasting for Pricing}

Pricing rules draw on demand forecasts, elasticity estimates, or both.
Zhang and Meng~\cite{zhang2025} model time-varying price elasticity
from retail time series, relaxing the constant-elasticity assumption
embedded in many static GLM specifications.
Bu~et~al.~\cite{bu2023} analyse offline demand learning when sales are
censored by stockouts, a common complication in retail transaction
data.
Ramos~et~al.~\cite{ramos2023} benchmark multivariate forecasters on
retail sales series at the SKU level.
Xia~et~al.~\cite{xia2023} generate synthetic retail demand with known
elasticities so that estimated parameters can be checked against
ground truth; we adopt that validation logic in our simulation
benchmark.

\subsection{Econometric Demand Models}

For count demand, a log-linked GLM remains a standard specification
when managers require interpretable own- and cross-price
effects~\cite{zhang2025}.
Safonov~\cite{safonov2024} contrasts neural demand estimators with
econometric models in retail and notes that flexible machine-learning
fit can underuse sparse within-product price variation that a GLM
isolates.
The implication for hybrid pricing is to treat econometric structure
as an auditable input to learning, not as the only admissible policy.

\subsection{RL for Pricing}

Apte~et~al.~\cite{apte2024} apply tabular Q-learning to retail pricing
without an explicit structural demand block in the agent.
Alamdar and Seifi~\cite{alamdar2024} extend deep Q-learning to joint
ordering and pricing decisions.
Cheung and Simchi-Levi~\cite{cheung2023} study nonstationary RL and
the cost of unrestricted early exploration---one motivation for
restricting actions while a demand model is still imprecise.
Zheng~et~al.~\cite{zheng2024} combine learned demand representations
with a dual-agent deep RL policy for pricing and replenishment; their
demand-learning layer is the closest architectural neighbour to our
GLM-guided agent.
When live price experiments are costly or infeasible, offline and
simulation-based evaluation protocols offer a practical
alternative~\cite{nambiar2022}.

Prior retail pricing studies typically pursue either econometric
optimisation from a fixed demand model or end-to-end RL with limited
structural transparency.
Few papers specify separate channels---state information, reward
shaping, and action restrictions---through which econometric estimates
enter an RL policy, then compare the resulting hybrid against GLM-only
and pure DQN baselines on a holdout window with known elasticities.
EG-DQN is intended to document that integration in simulation; we do
not present it as a deployment-ready pricing system.

% ── Step 4: Problem Formulation ──────────────────────────────
\section{Problem Formulation}
\label{sec:problem}

\subsection{Market Structure}

We consider a single retailer managing $n=3$ substitute products over a
discrete horizon of $T$~days, following multi-product retail pricing
formulations~\cite{rios2023}.
At each period $t \in \{1,\ldots,T\}$, the retailer observes state
information and sets a price for the focal product from a finite action
space.
Cross-product substitution is captured in the demand system below; RL
policies are estimated \emph{per product}, with rival prices entering
as inputs to the econometric model rather than as joint actions in a
single multi-product MDP.
The objective is a pricing policy $\pi : \mathcal{S} \to \mathcal{A}$
that maximises expected cumulative revenue:
\begin{equation}
  \pi^* = \arg\max_{\pi}\;
  \mathbb{E}\!\left[\,\sum_{t=1}^{T} p_{t} \cdot
  D_t(p_t, s_t)\right],
  \label{eq:objective}
\end{equation}
where $p_t$ and $D_t$ denote the price and realised demand of the focal
item on day~$t$.

\subsection{Demand Data-Generating Process}

Simulated demand follows a log-linear multiplicative structure grounded
in demand theory~\cite{kopalle2023, zhang2025}:
\begin{equation}
  D_{it} = B_i(t)\cdot S_i(t)\cdot
  \prod_{j=1}^{n}\!\left(\frac{p_{jt}}{\bar{p}_j}\right)^{\!\varepsilon_{ij}}
  + \eta_{it},
  \label{eq:dgp}
\end{equation}
where $B_i(t)$ is a time-varying base-demand component (trend),
$S_i(t)$ is a multiplicative seasonal index, $\varepsilon_{ij}$ is the
cross-price elasticity of product~$i$ with respect to product~$j$'s
price (own-price elasticity when $i=j$), $\bar{p}_j$ is a reference
price, and $\eta_{it}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_i^2)$ is i.i.d.\ Gaussian
noise.
Because $\log\,\mathbb{E}[D_{it}]$ is linear in $\log p_{jt}$, the
Poisson GLM with log-link used later is \emph{approximately} aligned
with the simulator; it is not treated as a correctly specified model
for the noise term or for all trend--seasonal components.

\subsection{Pricing as a Markov Decision Process}

The focal product's pricing problem is written as an MDP
$\mathcal{M} = (\mathcal{S}, \mathcal{A}, \mathcal{P}, \mathcal{R},
\gamma)$.

\smallskip\noindent\textbf{State space $\mathcal{S}$:}
$s_t \in \mathbb{R}^5$ comprises:
(i)~previous-period price;
(ii)~previous-period observed demand;
(iii)~day-of-year sine encoding annual seasonality;
(iv)~normalised time index $t/T$; and
(v)~the GLM-optimal price $p^*_{\text{GLM},t}$.
Component~(v) is the econometric signal that distinguishes EG-DQN from
pure DQN.

\smallskip\noindent\textbf{Action space $\mathcal{A}$:}
A discrete grid of $K=20$ prices on $[p_{\min},\,p_{\max}]$.
During early training, EG-DQN restricts feasible actions to a
neighbourhood of $p^*_{\text{GLM},t}$ (action masking).

\smallskip\noindent\textbf{Reward --- Pure DQN:}
\begin{equation}
  r_t = p_t \cdot \max\!\bigl(0,\,\hat{D}_t(p_t, s_t)\bigr),
  \label{eq:reward_dqn}
\end{equation}
where $\hat{D}_t$ is the GLM demand forecast used as a revenue proxy.

\smallskip\noindent\textbf{Reward --- EG-DQN:}
\begin{equation}
  r_t^{\text{EG}} = r_t \;+\;
  \lambda\cdot\exp\!\!\left(-\frac{|p_t - p^*_{\text{GLM},t}|}{\sigma_p}\right),
  \label{eq:reward_egdqn}
\end{equation}
with econometric weight $\lambda$ and price scale $\sigma_p$.
The bonus attracts the agent toward GLM-endorsed prices without fixing
the selected price.

\smallskip\noindent\textbf{Discount factor:}
$\gamma = 0.99$, consistent with daily pricing over finite training
episodes.

% ── Step 5: Econometric Demand Model ─────────────────────────
\section{Econometric Demand Model}
\label{sec:glm}

\subsection{Specification}

We estimate a separate Poisson GLM with log-link for each product
$i$. Count demand is natural for daily units sold; the log-link yields
interpretable own- and cross-price elasticities of the form common in
retail pricing research~\cite{kopalle2023, zhang2025}.
For product~$i$,
\begin{align}
  \log\bigl(\mathbb{E}[D_{it}]\bigr) =\;
  &\alpha_0
  + \underbrace{\alpha_1 \log p_{it}}_{\text{own elasticity}}
  + \sum_{j\neq i}\beta_j \log p_{jt} \nonumber\\
  &+ \gamma_1\,\text{trend}_t
  + \gamma_2\sin\!\!\left(\tfrac{2\pi\,\text{day}_t}{365}\right)
  + \gamma_3\,\text{weekend}_t,
  \label{eq:glm}
\end{align}
where $\alpha_1$ is the own-price elasticity, $\beta_j$ are cross-price
elasticities, and $\gamma_k$ capture trend, annual seasonality, and
weekend effects.
Coefficients are estimated by maximum likelihood on the training
window only; HC3-robust standard errors are reported.
Holdout forecasts and GLM-optimal prices use this fixed fit---we do not
refit online on the 120-day test window.
As noted in Section~\ref{sec:problem}, the log-linear DGP is only
approximately aligned with~\eqref{eq:glm}; time-varying elasticity is
outside the present specification~\cite{zhang2025}.

\subsection{Derived Econometric Signals}

Three GLM outputs feed the hybrid agent~\cite{safonov2024}:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Estimated elasticity}
    $\hat{\varepsilon}_i = \hat{\alpha}_1$.
    In simulation we compare $\hat{\varepsilon}_i$ to the known DGP
    own-price elasticity as an \emph{elasticity recovery} check, not as
    a claim of structural identification.
  \item \textbf{GLM-optimal price} $p^*_{\text{GLM},t}$.
    For each day~$t$ and covariate vector $\mathbf{x}_t$, we evaluate
    expected revenue $R(p)=p\cdot\hat{D}_i(p,\mathbf{x}_t)$ on the
    discrete price grid and set $p^*_{\text{GLM},t}=\arg\max_p R(p)$.
    This quantity enters the EG-DQN state, the early action mask, and
    the econometric reward bonus.
  \item \textbf{Forecast demand} $\hat{D}_{it}$.
    The fitted mean demand at the chosen price serves as a revenue
    proxy in~\eqref{eq:reward_dqn}, limiting the effect of day-to-day
    demand noise on the learning signal~\cite{bu2023}.
\end{enumerate}
Estimation accuracy and holdout forecast metrics are deferred to the
experimental results; this section defines only the signals passed to
the DQN.

% ── Step 6a–6b: RL Framework (network + training) ─────────────
\section{Reinforcement Learning Framework}
\label{sec:rl}

\subsection{Network Architecture}

Each product is assigned its own Deep Q-Network agent.
The policy network $Q(s,a;\theta)$ maps the five-dimensional state
vector defined in Section~\ref{sec:problem} to a Q-value for each of
$K=20$ discrete candidate prices.
The network has two hidden layers of 128 units with ReLU activations
and Glorot initialisation; parameters are updated in NumPy without a
deep-learning framework dependency, which keeps the simulation
reproducible on standard hardware.
Retail Q-learning studies motivate discrete-action pricing agents of
this form~\cite{apte2024, alamdar2024}.
Temporal-difference targets follow the standard DQN backup with a
separate target network $\theta^-$; the regression loss is Huber with
$\delta=1$ rather than squared error, which limits the influence of
occasional high-revenue transitions:
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{B}}
  \!\left[\ell_\delta\!\!\left(r + \gamma\max_{a'}
  Q(s',a';\theta^{-}) - Q(s,a;\theta)\right)\right].
  \label{eq:bellman}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Training Procedure}

Training proceeds for 300 episodes per product.
At each environment step the agent stores transitions
$(s_t,a_t,r_t,s_{t+1})$ in a replay buffer of capacity 10{,}000; a
minibatch of 64 transitions is drawn uniformly for each gradient
update.
The target network weights are copied from the online network every
15~episodes.
Action selection is $\varepsilon$-greedy with $\varepsilon$ decaying
from 1.0 to 0.05 over training; gradient norms are clipped at
$\ell_2=1.0$.
The learning rate is $10^{-3}$.
Pure DQN uses the reward in~\eqref{eq:reward_dqn}; EG-DQN variants
and econometric hyperparameters ($\lambda$, masking horizon) are
specified in the next subsection.

\subsection{EG-DQN Integration Mechanisms}

EG-DQN extends the base DQN with three channels through which the
fixed GLM fit from Section~\ref{sec:glm} enters learning.
Pure DQN omits all three; the ablation in the results section toggles
reward shaping while holding the remaining protocol fixed.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{State augmentation.}
    The state vector includes $p^*_{\text{GLM},t}$ at every step
    (component~(v) in Section~\ref{sec:problem}), giving the agent an
    explicit econometric price benchmark alongside lagged market
    variables.
  \item \textbf{Reward shaping.}
    Training rewards follow~\eqref{eq:reward_egdqn} with
    $\lambda=30.0$ and $\sigma_p$ set to the standard deviation of the
    discrete price grid.
    The exponential bonus is largest when the selected price coincides
    with $p^*_{\text{GLM},t}$ and decays smoothly with price distance.
  \item \textbf{Early action masking.}
    For episodes $e \le 30$, feasible actions are restricted to grid
    points within $\pm\sigma_p$ of $p^*_{\text{GLM},t}$, limiting
    exploratory prices while the replay buffer is still sparse.
    The full grid is available from episode~31 onward.
    Restricting early exploration in nonstationary settings can reduce
    the cost of uninformed trial prices~\cite{cheung2023}.
\end{itemize}

Algorithm~\ref{alg:egdqn} summarises one training episode; the outer
loop runs $E=300$ episodes per product with the hyperparameters in
Section~\ref{sec:rl}.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{EG-DQN Training (single episode)}
\label{alg:egdqn}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Fitted GLM $\mathcal{G}$, environment $\mathcal{M}$,
         replay buffer $\mathcal{B}$, weights $\theta$, target
         $\theta^-$, exploration rate $\varepsilon$
\State $s_0 \gets \textsc{Reset}()$;
       compute $p^*_{\text{GLM},0}$ from $\mathcal{G}$
\For{step $t = 0, 1, \ldots$ until episode end}
  \If{current episode $e \le 30$}
    $\mathcal{A}_t \gets \{p \in \text{grid} :
      |p - p^*_{\text{GLM},t}| \le \sigma_p\}$
  \Else
    $\mathcal{A}_t \gets$ full price grid
  \EndIf
  \State $a_t \gets \varepsilon\text{-greedy}\bigl(
    Q(s_t,\cdot;\theta), \mathcal{A}_t\bigr)$
  \State Execute $a_t$; observe $r_t^{\text{EG}}$
    from~\eqref{eq:reward_egdqn}
  \State $s_{t+1} \gets$ next state; store
    $(s_t,a_t,r_t^{\text{EG}},s_{t+1})$ in $\mathcal{B}$
  \If{$|\mathcal{B}| \ge 64$}
    \State Sample minibatch from $\mathcal{B}$;
      update $\theta$ via~\eqref{eq:bellman} with gradient clip
  \EndIf
  \State $s_t \gets s_{t+1}$
\EndFor
\State Decay $\varepsilon$; if $e \bmod 15 = 0$ then $\theta^- \gets \theta$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

% ── Step 7: Simulation Environment ─────────────────────────────
\section{Simulation Environment}
\label{sec:simulation}

\subsection{Design and Calibration}

We evaluate EG-DQN in a controlled synthetic market rather than a live
retail test.
Simulation allows known ground-truth elasticities, a fixed holdout
protocol, and reproducible comparison of pricing policies under identical
demand trajectories---an evaluation mode used for offline and retail RL
studies when field experimentation is costly or infeasible~\cite{nambiar2022,
xia2023, xia2024, chen2023}.
We do not treat the synthetic setting as a substitute for real-store
validation; it is a design and benchmark study.

The simulator generates daily demand for $n=3$ substitute products over
$T=1{,}460$ days (1~January~2021 -- 30~December~2024).
The final 120~days form an out-of-sample holdout; the main revenue
comparison in the results section uses Item~1 on that window.
Demand parameters are calibrated to statistical properties typical of
large retail benchmarks (M5 / Walmart-style series and related synthetic
generators~\cite{xia2023}): prices on a $\$6$--$\$18$ range, demand
coefficient of variation of approximately~$0.25$, and clear annual
seasonality.
All stochastic draws and training runs use seed~$42$.

Environment components are as follows.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Trend.}
    Base demand grows linearly,
    $B_i(t)=\mathrm{base}_i\cdot(1+g_i\cdot t/T)$, with base levels
    $(50,\,40,\,60)$ units/day for Items~1--3.
  \item \textbf{Seasonality.}
    An annual sinusoid (amplitudes $12$, $8$, $15$) plus an $8\%$
    weekend uplift.
  \item \textbf{Price paths.}
    Step-function list prices with roughly $60$ discrete changes per
    product over four years, mimicking intermittent promotions.
  \item \textbf{Noise.}
    $\eta_{it}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_i^2)$ with
    $\sigma=(4.0,\,3.5,\,5.0)$.
\end{itemize}

Table~\ref{tab:elasticity_matrix} reports the cross-price elasticity
matrix used in~\eqref{eq:dgp}.
All own-price elasticities satisfy $|\varepsilon_{ii}|>1$, so demand is
elastic at the reference prices and a revenue-maximising price exists in
the continuous problem.
As noted in Section~\ref{sec:problem}, the DGP is only approximately
aligned with the Poisson log-link GLM; elasticities recovered by the GLM
are therefore a sanity check against known simulator parameters, not
external validation.

\subsection{Hyperparameters}

Table~\ref{tab:hyperparams} lists the simulation and training settings
used for all reported runs.
The discrete action set comprises $K=20$ prices spanning $\$6.0$ to
$\$16.0$, matching the grid used in the experimental notebook.
Learning parameters follow a standard DQN recipe; the econometric
penalty weight is $\lambda=30.0$ with $\sigma_p$ equal to the standard
deviation of the price grid.

\subsection{Baseline Strategies}

Five pricing strategies are compared on the same simulated trajectories
and the same 120-day holdout for Item~1.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Fixed-Price (FP).}
    Charge the reference price $\bar{p}$ every day.
    This is a naive non-adaptive floor.
  \item \textbf{Rule-Based (RB).}
    Set price to $1.15\times$ the trailing 7-day mean of observed
    prices.
    The rule is a simple retailer heuristic, not an optimised policy.
  \item \textbf{GLM-Optimal (GLM).}
    Each day, charge $p^*_{\text{GLM},t}$ from the fitted Poisson model
    (Section~\ref{sec:glm}).
    This is the pure econometric benchmark on the discrete grid.
  \item \textbf{Pure DQN.}
    The network and training procedure of Section~\ref{sec:rl} without
    state augmentation of $p^*_{\text{GLM},t}$, without reward shaping,
    and without action masking.
  \item \textbf{EG-DQN.}
    The full hybrid: all three integration mechanisms of
    Section~\ref{sec:rl}.
\end{itemize}
Holdout evaluation uses realised simulator demand under each policy's
chosen prices.
Statistical comparisons of daily revenue series follow in the results
section; the present section only fixes the protocol.

\subsection{Ablation Study}

We isolate the holdout contribution of econometric reward shaping in a
three-configuration ablation on Item~1.
Each variant is trained for $200$ episodes under the same simulator and
evaluated on the 120-day holdout; Table~\ref{tab:ablation} and
Figure~\ref{fig:ablation} summarise the outcome.

Pure DQN attains \$173{,}830 on the holdout window.
Adding the GLM-based reward bonus (DQN\,+\,Reward) raises test revenue to
\$226{,}764 ($+30.5\%$ relative to Pure DQN in this ablation run).
The full EG-DQN configuration---state augmentation and early action masking
included---matches DQN\,+\,Reward exactly on holdout revenue (\$226{,}764,
$+30.5\%$).
In this simulation, therefore, econometric \emph{reward shaping} accounts
for the principal holdout improvement over pure DQN; the additional
integration channels do not increase out-of-sample revenue beyond the
reward-only variant.
That pattern is consistent with the view that econometric structure is
most informative as a training signal rather than as a guarantee of
hybrid dominance over a GLM-only benchmark~\cite{safonov2024}.
Separate ablations of state augmentation alone and action masking alone
are left to future work.

one-sided Mann--Whitney $U$ tests ($H_1$: EG-DQN $>$ baseline) on the

difference use $B=2{,}000$ resamples.

Pure DQN is omitted because the exported daily series tied at the wrong
revenue level in the notebook log and should be re-tested before any

Relative to fixed-price and rule-based policies, EG-DQN yields
significantly higher daily revenue ($p<0.001$ in both cases).

of the hybrid design in this simulation.

\subsection{Rolling Elasticity Estimates}

EG-DQN conditions learning on GLM outputs that are re-estimated as data
accumulate.
As a descriptive check on Item~1, we refit the Poisson log-link model on
rolling twelve-month windows (advance $30$~days per step) and record the
own-price elasticity at each window end.
Figure~\ref{fig:rolling_elasticity} plots the point estimate and an
approximate $95\%$ interval against the simulator parameter
($\varepsilon=-1.50$).

Over 2022--2024 the estimate moves with window composition: values lie
between roughly $-1.35$ and $-1.7$, so demand stays in the elastic range
($|\varepsilon|>1$) throughout, but the path is not flat.
The dashed truth line sits inside some intervals and outside others,
which is plausible when prices change in steps and each window mixes
different promotional regimes.
We read this figure as evidence that the econometric input to EG-DQN is
\emph{usable} in simulation---not that elasticity is fixed, nor that the
GLM prior would remain dependable in a live store without revalidation.

% ── Step 9: Discussion ────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Methodological Implications}

This simulation compares econometric and model-free pricing on common
ground rather than treating them as mutually exclusive toolkits.
GLM-optimal pricing and EG-DQN reach the same holdout revenue on
Item~1, which suggests that econometric \emph{reward shaping} can steer
a DQN toward GLM-endorsed prices when the discrete grid makes those
prices optimal.
The ablation points in the same direction: holdout gains over pure DQN
appear once the GLM bonus is switched on, while state augmentation and
masking do not add further test revenue in this run.
We do not read that pattern as hybrid dominance over econometrics; it
is a design observation about where GLM signals mattered under our
protocol.

The distinction between model-based structure and sequential learning
still matters for how one would build a pricing system.
Econometric estimates supply interpretable elasticities and a price
benchmark; RL supplies a policy that can, in principle, adapt when
relationships drift~\cite{apte2024}.
Prior work on nonstationary RL notes that restricting early exploration
can limit the cost of uninformed price trials~\cite{cheung2023}; our
masking and shaping mechanisms follow that logic, though we did not
establish faster convergence or lower variance from the loss logs
(\texttt{Loss=nan}).

Large Cohen's $d$ values against fixed-price and rule-based baselines
(Table~\ref{tab:significance}) should be interpreted cautiously: those
policies held nearly constant prices on the holdout, so daily revenue
series are far apart for reasons that mix policy design with adaptive
skill.

\subsection{Limitations}

Several constraints bound the findings.

\smallskip\noindent\textbf{Simulation scope.}
All evidence comes from a synthetic three-product environment with known
elasticities, following the offline evaluation logic used when field
tests are costly~\cite{nambiar2022, xia2024}.
We do not claim transfer to a live retailer.

\smallskip\noindent\textbf{Holdout tie and corner pricing.}
GLM-optimal and EG-DQN policies coincide at the grid minimum
(\$6) on every holdout day, so the experiment does not explore interior
price dynamics and the hybrid adds no out-of-sample revenue over
GLM-only pricing.
Percentage lifts versus fixed-price therefore partly reflect that corner
outcome, not a general ranking of methods.

\smallskip\noindent\textbf{DGP--model alignment.}
The simulator is approximately consistent with a Poisson log-link GLM,
which may favour econometric-guided agents relative to settings with
different demand geometry.
The GLM recovers own-price elasticities reasonably well yet can
misstate demand \emph{levels} (Figure~\ref{fig:elasticity_validation}),
and holdout forecasts show a slight mean over-forecast
(Figure~\ref{fig:forecast}).

\smallskip\noindent\textbf{Protocol choices.}
DQN training is per item, not joint across products; actions are limited
to $K=20$ prices; a single random seed and one 120-day holdout window
were used.
Training TD losses were not usable (\texttt{nan}), so learning dynamics
are under-reported.
The rule-based heuristic underperformed fixed-price in this run---a
weakness of that heuristic, not a general statement about rule-based
pricing.
The reward penalty $\lambda$ is not normalised to revenue units.

\smallskip\noindent\textbf{Incomplete ablation.}
State-only and mask-only variants were not estimated; rolling elasticity
estimates drift over time (Figure~\ref{fig:rolling_elasticity}), so a
fixed GLM prior may need more frequent updating in practice than our
simulation assumed.

\subsection{Path to Deployment}

Operational use would require steps beyond this study.
A credible path would follow simulation-to-field pipelines discussed in
retail RL benchmarking~\cite{xia2024} and field-tested revenue management
work~\cite{chen2023}: calibrate a simulator or historical replay
environment from store data, re-estimate demand models on a rolling basis
(given time-varying elasticity in retail~\cite{zhang2025}), run fuller
ablations of each EG-DQN channel, and only then consider controlled
store experiments.
Until those steps are completed, EG-DQN should be read as a documented
hybrid \emph{design} and evaluation protocol, not as a deployment
recommendation.
